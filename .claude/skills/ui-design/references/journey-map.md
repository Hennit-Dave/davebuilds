# Journey Mapping — End-to-End Experience

# Journey Map

Map the end-to-end user experience to find where it breaks, where it delights, and where design investment will have the most impact.

## What a Journey Map Is (and Isn't)

A journey map is a strategic tool, not a task flow. It captures the user's **experience** — their emotions, questions, and frustrations — across a broad arc of time, not just the steps they take inside your product.

A user flow shows what buttons they click. A journey map shows how they feel along the way.

Use journey maps when:
- You're making strategic decisions about where to invest design effort
- You're designing onboarding and want to understand the full acquisition-to-value arc
- You've discovered a drop-off in analytics and want to understand the experiential context
- You're aligning a team around a shared understanding of the user experience

## Journey Map Components

### Persona
Which user is this journey for? The journey should be persona-specific — a power user's journey through your product looks completely different from a first-time user's.

### Scenario
What goal is the user trying to accomplish? What are the start and end points of this journey?
- Start: the moment the user first becomes aware of the need this product addresses
- End: the moment they've achieved their goal (or given up, or moved to advocacy)

### Stages
5–7 high-level phases the user moves through. Don't just map "using the product" — map the whole arc including pre- and post-product moments.

Common stage frameworks:
- **Awareness → Consideration → Purchase → Onboarding → Regular use → Advocacy**
- **Trigger → Research → Evaluation → Activation → Habit → Recommendation**
- **Problem → Discovery → Exploration → Decision → Setup → Ongoing use**

### Per-Stage Content

For each stage, document:

**User goals**: What is the user trying to accomplish in this stage?

**Actions**: What are they actually doing? (searching, reading reviews, signing up, contacting support)

**Touchpoints and channels**: Where are they interacting? (your app, email, support chat, competitor site, word of mouth, app store)

**Thoughts and questions**: What's going through their head? ("Is this trustworthy?" "How long will this take?" "What happens to my data?")

**Emotional state**: How does the user feel? Rate on a scale from very negative (-2) to very positive (+2). This creates the emotional curve.

**Pain points**: Where does friction, confusion, or frustration occur?

**Opportunity areas**: What could be designed or improved to address the pain point or capitalize on a positive moment?

### Emotional Curve
The sequence of emotional states across stages, visualized as a curve. Look for:
- **Cliff drops**: Stages where emotion drops sharply — these are crisis points
- **Valleys**: Extended periods of negative experience
- **Peaks**: Moments of delight that could be amplified
- **Flat zones**: Neutral but uninspiring stretches where differentiation could happen

### Moments of Truth
2–4 make-or-break moments where the experience is won or lost. These are not the same as pain points — a moment of truth can be positive or negative. They're moments where user perception of your product crystallizes.

Common moments of truth:
- First impression (the moment they land on your site or open your app)
- First value moment (when they first see why this was worth signing up)
- First frustration (the first time something doesn't work as expected)
- Habit formation (the moment the product becomes part of their routine)

## How to Build a Journey Map

**Step 1: Ground in research**
A journey map built from assumptions is a fiction map. Base each stage on real data: user interviews, session recordings, support tickets, NPS comments, analytics. Note confidence levels — "we know this from 10 interviews" vs. "we're assuming this."

**Step 2: Define scope**
- Confirm the persona
- Define the scenario (specific goal, not "using the product")
- Set the start and end points

**Step 3: Identify stages**
Draft 5–7 stages. Don't over-granulate (too many stages = a flow chart, not a journey map).

**Step 4: Map each stage**
Fill in goals, actions, touchpoints, thoughts, emotions, and pain points for each stage. Be specific — "they feel frustrated" is weaker than "they feel frustrated because they can't tell if the action succeeded."

**Step 5: Draw the emotional curve**
Plot the emotional ratings across stages. Where does it drop? Where does it peak? The shape of this curve tells you where to focus.

**Step 6: Identify moments of truth**
Mark 2–4 stages or transitions that are highest-stakes — where the user is most likely to abandon, delight, or convert.

**Step 7: Prioritize opportunities**
From all the pain points and opportunity areas, identify the top 3–5 to pursue, ranked by:
- Emotional impact (does addressing this move the curve significantly?)
- Reach (how many users hit this pain point?)
- Feasibility (can we realistically improve this?)

**Step 8: Note research gaps**
Where did you fill in stages with assumptions? Flag these explicitly. They're the highest-priority research questions for validation.

## Output Format

```
Journey Map: [Persona] — [Scenario]
Date: [When built]
Research basis: [What data informed this]

Stage 1: [Name]
├── Goals: [...]
├── Actions: [...]
├── Touchpoints: [...]
├── Thoughts: "[...]"
├── Emotion: [-2 to +2] — [descriptor]
├── Pain points: [...]
└── Opportunities: [...]

[Repeat for each stage]

EMOTIONAL CURVE: [-1, -1, 0, +1, +2, +1, +2] (plot as diagram)

MOMENTS OF TRUTH:
1. [Stage/transition]: [Why it's critical]
2. [...]

TOP 5 OPPORTUNITIES (prioritized):
1. [Opportunity] — Impact: [H/M/L] Reach: [H/M/L] Feasibility: [H/M/L]
2. [...]

RESEARCH GAPS (assumptions to validate):
- [Stage]: [What we assumed, what we need to learn]
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Making it too granular**: A journey map with 20 stages is a flow chart. Keep it to 5–7 high-level phases.
- **Only mapping inside the product**: The most important stages often happen before the user opens your app (discovery, decision) or after they close it (reflection, sharing).
- **Building without research**: A journey map built on assumptions gives false confidence. Label your assumptions.
- **No emotional curve**: Without the emotional data, you just have a list of steps — not a journey map.
- **Too many moments of truth**: If everything is a moment of truth, nothing is. Pick 2–4 maximum.
- **Forgetting the opportunities**: The point isn't to document the problem — it's to identify what to design.
